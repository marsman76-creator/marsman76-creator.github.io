Georgia early vote: who is turning out

Georgians have cast 1.75 million ballots so far, 6.6% more than at the same point in 2022 and 44% of that year's entire vote. Most are habitual early voters: 70% voted early in 2022, while 18% did not vote in the 2022 general election at all. Combining the polls with the early vote, the Senate race strongly favors Ossoff and the governor's race is a toss-up.

The polls

The latest poll from each pollster with fieldwork since 14 September, all of likely voters. In the spirit of the New York Times' "select pollster" standard (nonpartisan sponsors with a record of accuracy), each poll counts in proportion to its 538 rating, partisan-aligned pollsters count half, and older polls fade with a 28-day half-life. Each margin is also corrected for the pollster's historical lean against other polls in the same races. Nonpartisan pollsters carry 68% of the Senate weight and 68% of the governor weight. Left out: Rasmussen Reports, which 538 dropped from its averages in March 2024 for failing its pollster standards; Wedgewood Polls, whose numbers were withdrawn after the outfit was found to be spreading false information; and ActiVote, an app panel that Silver Bulletin does not count as a scientific poll.

How this projection works

  • Polls. Six Senate polls and five governor polls. A poll's weight is its 538 grade divided by 3 (unrated pollsters count as 1 star), halved if the pollster is partisan-aligned (marked R or D in Wikipedia's Georgia poll tables, sponsored by a partisan outlet, or counted by 538 as doing mostly partisan work), then halved for every 28 days since the poll ended. Lean corrections come from 538's poll archive for 2016–2022: each pollster's average error minus the average of all polls in the same races, shrunk toward zero when it has few polls. Aligned pollsters with no record get 2 points, the typical lean of Republican-aligned pollsters that have one. The weighted, corrected average is D+10.3 for Senate and R+0.9 for governor; a plain average of the same polls would be D+9.3 and R+1.6. 538 closed in 2025, so these are its last published ratings.
  • Lean against other polls, not against results. The correction measures how each pollster runs compared with other polls of the same races, the usual practice in polling averages. Correcting each poll for its average miss against actual results instead would also build in the whole industry's misses, which in 2016 and 2020 underestimated Republicans; that version gives D+8.9 for Senate and R+2.2 for governor before the early-vote adjustment.
  • Early vote and race. The electorate is modelled as five groups. Black voters' preferences come from the polls that report both candidates' shares among them (Quantus Insights (Ossoff 89–7, Bottoms 82–10)); Hispanic and Asian voters' from the 2022 exit poll for the same office; voters of other or unknown race are assumed to split like everyone else; and white voters' share is whatever makes the 2022 electorate (about 28% Black) match the poll average. The adjustment changes only the Black share, because that is the one group with a 2022 early-vote benchmark at this stage. Each extra point moves the Senate margin about 1.06 points and the governor's margin about 1.12 points toward the Democrat. The 2022 benchmark stays the 29 October 2022 snapshot, so as this year's count moves past that stage the comparison gets looser.
  • The key assumption. "Polls alone" is right if pollsters already expect a Blacker electorate than 2022's, or if this year's edge is only Black voters casting ballots earlier. "Hold" is right if the polls are weighted to a 2022-like electorate and the edge is new turnout. The truth is likely in between, which is why the headline uses the midpoint.
  • Not modelled. This year's early vote is also younger and more female than the 2022 benchmarks suggest, but there are no verified same-stage 2022 figures to measure that against. Party is not recorded on Georgia's voter file.
  • Updates. A scheduled task rebuilds this page twice a day through Election Day with the newest Georgia Votes file and any new qualifying polls.
  • Uncertainty. The chances assume the final margin can miss the projection with a standard error of 5 points, roughly the size of recent polling misses in Senate and governor races. They are estimates for this page, not an official forecast.
  • Other views. RealClearPolitics' Senate average is Ossoff +8.2 (polls from 21 September to 17 October). For governor, Cook, Inside Elections, Sabato's Crystal Ball and Decision Desk HQ rated the race a toss-up in early October; Silver Bulletin had it leaning Democratic.

Reading the map

  • "Of 2022 vote" compares this year's ballots so far with each county's whole 2022 general-election turnout, so it rises toward and past 100% as voting goes on.
  • "Of registered" divides ballots so far by the county's registered voters. Chattahoochee, which is mostly the Army's Fort Benning, has the lowest rate.
  • "2024 result" is the certified presidential margin, shown for reference so turnout can be read against it. It says nothing about how this year's voters are voting.
  • Tap or hover a county for its numbers. The county table on the first tab has every figure.

How to read this

  • Counts come from the Secretary of State's daily absentee voter file as compiled by Georgia Votes, file of 25 October 2026, 6:04 a.m. Eastern. County and district rows add up exactly to the statewide totals. Counties upload on their own schedules, so the latest day can be slightly incomplete.
  • "Ballots" are in-person early votes plus accepted mail ballots. "Same point in 2022" is Georgia Votes' comparison with 2022's early vote at the matching stage.
  • 2022 voting history comes from the state's voter history files: early (in person or by mail), election day, or no 2022 general-election vote.
  • This page has the Texas tracker's layout but not its partisan model. That page scores every early voter by a modelled 2024 vote; Georgia's public turnout data is aggregated by county, district and group, so it supports turnout and who is voting, not how they lean.
  • 2024 county results are the certified presidential returns. They describe places, not this year's voters.
